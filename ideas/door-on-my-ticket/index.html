The Door Was Printed On My Ticket

A Colts game, a bike with no manual, and the 60-second trick that makes AI ask you the right questions.

By Jimmy Davis · 5 minute read

ROUGH DRAFT. This is an early version we are still working on. Pictures and wording will change. Tell us what lands and what loses you: hello@2guysandagenie.com

The fastest way into a stadium with 60,000 people in it was printed on my own ticket. I needed AI to point it out.

That's not really a football story.

It's the easiest way I know to start using AI. And it starts with a bike.

Stay with me.

You Learn This Like A Bike, Not Like The Law

Here's the first thing I want you to know.

AI isn't something you learn the way a lawyer memorizes the law. You don't read the whole book first and then go try it.

It's more like riding a bike. You get the feel for it by doing it.

Nobody ever learned to ride a bike from the manual. You got on. You wobbled. Somebody held the seat for a few feet. Then one day you were just riding.

AI works the same way. And everybody ends up riding a little differently. My business partner Paul has his way. Our partner Daegan has his. I have mine. It kind of merges with you.

So if you feel a little lost at first, good. That means you're learning.

So instead of a manual, let me show you me, wobbling, at a Colts game.

Sixty Thousand People And One Question

A Sunday in November. Amy and I went to the Colts game.

If you've been to one, you know the problem. Sixty thousand people. Doors on every side of the building. Pick the wrong one and you're shuffling through a packed hallway for ten minutes.

So I snapped a picture of our tickets. I talked out loud for about ten seconds into the little Bee recorder I wear on my shirt. And I asked Claude (an AI app, a cousin of ChatGPT) one thing:

What's the best door? Think it through.

It said: the South Gate.

And here's the crazy part. The answer was printed on my own ticket. In tiny letters. I'd never have seen it.

Then it explained why. The door closest to downtown is the door everybody from downtown uses.

Closest isn't fastest.

Later, at the end of the third quarter, I walked back early to get the car so I could pick up Amy and her parents.

When a game ends, all 60,000 people pour out at once, and every Uber in Indianapolis lines up on the same street.

So while I walked, I talked again. Claude said: not by the stadium. One block past the Uber line, out front of a hotel. Leave with five minutes left.

And it wrote the text for me to send Amy.

No circling. No standing in the cold. No ten texts of "where are you?"

(Yes, I also asked it if it would remember I was nice to it when the robots take over. It said I have protected status. I'm choosing to believe it.)

What I Actually Did (Which Was Almost Nothing)

Look at what I really did, both times.

I didn't type a long, clever question. I didn't download a special app. I didn't take a class.

I talked. I gave it my real situation: a picture of the ticket, where we parked, what time it was. And I asked it to think it through, instead of just looking something up.

I did all of that just by talking. That's where AI is right now.

That's the whole move. Talk to it like you'd talk to a smart friend who happens to know a little about everything. Tell it what's really going on. Let it think.

Then You Start Seeing Blocks Everywhere

Anybody ever play Tetris?

People who play it for hours say the game follows them around. They close their eyes and see falling blocks. They look at a parking lot and think, "that car would slide right into that gap."

It's called the Tetris effect. Do something enough and your brain starts seeing the world through it.

That's me with AI now. I see it everywhere.

The invoice you keep forgetting to send. The review you never answered. The customer you meant to call back.

Every one of those is a block, with a spot it fits.

You don't have to find all the spots today. You just need your first one.

Try It Before Your Coffee Gets Cold

Here's the trick we did with the whole room at our AI night in Fishers on Nov 9. It takes about a minute.

Open ChatGPT or Claude on your phone. Tap the little microphone. (The microphone, not the sound waves. If it starts talking out loud, just tap the X.)

Now vent. For one minute, tell it what's eating your week. Like you're complaining to a friend over coffee. Messy is fine. Rambling is fine. Just leave out client names and account numbers.

Then, before you hit send, add one line.

Steal This

The vent trick (one minute, any AI)

  1. Tap the microphone and vent about your week for one minute.
  2. At the end, add this line, word for word:

"Before you try to solve anything, ask me questions one at a time until you're 95 percent sure you understand my situation. Don't guess."

  1. Hit send. Answer its question like you're texting a friend.

(I borrowed this one from Allie K. Miller, who teaches business people how to use AI.)

Here's what happens.

Without that line, most AI does what a nervous new employee does. It jumps straight to a list of generic tips for a business it has never seen.

With that line, it has to slow down and get to know you first.

So instead of ten tips, you get one question back. And when I do this, that first question is the kind a good consultant would ask me. Not "have you tried a planner?" A question about my situation.

That's the moment it clicks for most people. It's not a search box. It's a conversation.

The Only Requirement

So where does all of this begin?

Not tech skills. Not money. Not a team.

Curiosity. That's the only requirement.

That night I asked the room, "Who here can be curious?" Every hand could go up. Then you're in.

If you just tried the vent trick, you already did it. You're already using AI. You didn't type a single fancy instruction. You just talked.

The Hard Part Is The First Try

One more thing, and it matters more than it sounds.

The first try is the hardest part. It's a little like a rocket: getting off the ground takes almost everything. That first wobbly minute, feeling silly talking to your phone in the truck, is the launch.

Once you're up, it gets easier every day. (We'll fly that rocket all the way in the last article of this series.)

The door was printed on my ticket the whole time. I just had to ask.

Your first answer is probably sitting in your week right now. Go ask.
