Off The Ground

A moon rocket, a 32-year-old back at his parents' house, and the one question to ask anybody who wants to help you with AI.

By Jimmy Davis · 6 minute read

ROUGH DRAFT. This is an early version we are still working on. Pictures and wording will change. Tell us what lands and what loses you: hello@2guysandagenie.com

The rocket that took us to the moon was 36 stories tall. Almost all of it was fuel.

About ninety percent of its weight.

The part that actually came home? A little piece on top, about eleven feet tall.

And it burned most of that fuel in under three minutes.

Why? Gravity. Getting off the ground takes almost everything.

Remember the end of No. 1, when I said the first try is the launch? This is the rest of that rocket. I promised we'd fly it in the last article. This is the last article.

Why Week One Feels Like A Lot

If you've tried AI and thought "that was a lot of effort for not much," good. Nothing's wrong with you.

That's gravity.

The first few times feel clumsy. You don't know what to say. You feel a little silly talking to your phone in the truck. You get an answer that's almost right and you're not sure what to do with it.

That's the loud, shaky, burn-all-the-fuel part. It's supposed to feel like that.

Most people quit right there. Right at the bottom, with the engines roaring and the rocket barely moving.

Once It's Up, Nothing Slows It Down

Here's the other half, and it's the half nobody tells you.

We sent Voyager out in 1977. It's still going. Almost fifty years later.

Out there, there's nothing to slow it down.

A rocket has two lives. The short, expensive, shaky one where it fights to get up. And the long, quiet one where it flies.

AI is the same. The hard part is the beginning. After that, every week is easier than the last.

What Gravity Felt Like For Me

I know the shaky part pretty well. Just not with AI.

By the time I was 32, everything I'd built was gone. All of it.

I'd promised more than I could deliver. More websites than I could ever build. And it all came apart.

I moved back into my parents' house. I didn't want anybody to see me. I basically disappeared.

And here's the thing I think about all the time. If I'd had AI back then, I could have built most of what I promised. In days.

Sorry. I get a little excited.

But that's why I do this. Back then I had the ideas. What I didn't have was enough hands. You can have what I didn't have. Right now.

How I Actually Got Off The Ground

So how did I go from "huh, neat" to "I just talk and it goes"? Looking back, it happened in four steps.

1. I used it. Like everybody. Asked it questions. Wobbled. (That's No. 1.)

2. I fed it what I know. Remember No. 2, every book on every shelf, squeezed into your phone? Once I saw that, I started handing it my shelf too. Our notes. Our calls. Twenty years of direct response (that's the kind of advertising where you can count exactly who answered). Everything.

3. I connected it to the tools I already use. The calendar, the email, the phone. Not new stuff. My stuff.

4. Somewhere in there, it started building on itself.

That last one has a name. Rocket people call it escape velocity: the speed where you stop falling back down. Every new thing I taught it made the next thing easier.

Getting off the ground took almost everything. Once it was up, it flew.

And now? I just talk, and it goes.

Here's what those steps look like in a normal shop. Use it: vent to it for a minute about your week. Feed it: your price sheet, how you answer the phone, the five questions every customer asks. Connect it: let it work with the calendar and phone you already have. You don't need all four tonight. Most people stop at step one. Step two is where it starts to feel like yours.

Think of it this way. A regular chat is a meeting: it forgets. A chat that knows your shop is a desk: it remembers. One that does the job on its own is an employee. This box gets you a desk.

Steal This

Feed it your shop (10 minutes, tonight)

Open ChatGPT or Claude. Paste in, or just read out loud:

  • What you sell, and what it usually costs
  • The five questions customers ask you most, with your real answers
  • How you sound when you answer the phone on a good day

Then say: "This is my business. Read all of it. Before you help me with anything, ask me three questions about it, one at a time."

Save that chat. Next time you need an email, a reply to a review, or an estimate, ask in that chat. It already knows your shop.

Why Bother? The Business Is The Vehicle

Here's a question I asked the room at our Nov 9 night: why do we even have a business?

My answer: we're not really trying to build a business. We're trying to build a life we love. The business is just the vehicle.

Try this. Open the notes app on your phone. Nobody sees it but you. Write three numbers.

Number one: what does it take for every bill to be paid, so you never worry about money?

Number two: what would it take to be free? To do whatever you want on a Tuesday?

Number three: your dream number.

Now here's the catch. If you hit your dream number working eighty-hour weeks, you didn't build the life. You built a job that owns you.

That's the real reason to get off the ground. Not to do more. To get your Tuesdays back.

Three Honest Roads From Here

From here, every owner takes one of three roads. I'll be straight about all three.

Road one: do it yourself. This is what I did, and you can too. The good: you own the know-how. You don't need anybody. The catch: it's a long road. It took me twenty years, a lot of money, and a lot of mistakes. And you'd be doing it on your own. (The good news: the tools are way better now than when I started, and these ten articles are a decent pair of boots.)

Road two: have someone do it all for you. Honestly? That can be a smart choice. You don't have to think about it. The catch: it's the most expensive road, and you're separated from it. Something's working and you don't really know why. And if that one person disappears, so does it.

Road three: do it together, with someone who knows how. They build it. You steer. You own it. The catch: you still have to show up and make the calls, maybe an hour a week.

Full disclosure: road three is the one Paul and I walk with people. So weigh my opinion on it with that in mind.

Which one's right depends on what kind of person you are. Some people love figuring it out. Some people never want to touch it. Some want to own it and have somebody beside them. All three get you off the ground.

The Test For Anyone Selling You AI (Us Included)

Whichever road you pick, you're going to meet people who want to help you with AI. Lots of them.

Here's the test. Do they ask about your business first?

If they push a tool before they understand you, walk away.

That goes for us too. If I ever start telling you which tool to buy before I've asked about your customers, call me on it.

It's the same rule we gave the AI back in No. 1: ask questions until you understand my situation. Don't guess. Good help works like a good genie.

Ohana

Anybody see Lilo and Stitch? Ohana means family. And family means nobody gets left behind.

That's how Paul and I want AI to land in Fishers. Owners helping owners. Nobody gets left behind.

So here's the thing.

Remember that rocket at the top of this page? Thirty-six stories, almost all fuel, and one little piece on top that came home.

All that fuel was only ever there to carry the little piece on top.

That's you.

If you've read even a few of these articles, you've already burned through the hardest fuel. The weird first try. The "what is this thing, actually?" The feeling of being behind.

You have the genie now too. It's on your phone.

You're off the ground. From here, it gets easier every single day.
