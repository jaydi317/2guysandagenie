The Exit Moved

A McDonald's off the highway, a nine-dollar name, and the question your next customer is asking their phone right now.

By Jimmy Davis · 6 minute read

ROUGH DRAFT. This is an early version we are still working on. Pictures and wording will change. Tell us what lands and what loses you: hello@2guysandagenie.com

Imagine a genie showed up at your business tomorrow morning. Three wishes.

You're smart, so your first wish is a million more wishes.

Nice try. The genie has heard that one.

So, three real wishes. What are they?

Here's my guess at yours. Customers coming from everywhere, not just word of mouth. Your evenings back. And wish number one: when somebody asks their phone who's the best in town at what you do, it says your name.

Hold on to wish number one. This whole article is about it.

(If you've been reading these in order, the first four were about what AI is. This is where we put it to work. And we start with the oldest problem in business: getting picked.)

Nobody Thinks McDonald's Is The Best Burger

You've heard of a place called McDonald's, right?

Now be honest. Nobody would call McDonald's the best burger in the world.

But picture this. You're driving up I-69. You're hungry. What's the first thing you see off the exit?

A gas station. And a McDonald's.

They're right there when you need them. Most of the time they're the only choice. Unless you want gas station sushi.

So you pick McDonald's.

Not because they're the best. Because they were there, and you could find them.

Found, Then Chosen

Here's a hard question. Why do most small businesses fail?

(Quick number, then we move on. The Bureau of Labor Statistics says about half are gone within five years. If you've been at it longer than that, you've already beaten the odds. Give yourself a hand. Seriously.)

Most people think businesses fail because the product wasn't good enough. Or a competitor was better.

That's not it. Most of the owners I meet are great at what they do.

They fail for one of two reasons.

One: people stopped finding them.

Two: people found them, and chose someone else.

Found. And chosen. That's the whole game.

McDonald's wins "found" by standing at the exit. Simple as that.

So here's the question for your business: where's the exit now?

The Exit Moved

It used to be, you needed to be the sign at the exit.

Then you needed to be on Google.

Now people just ask their phone. "Who's the best plumber in Fishers?"

And AI gives them one name. Maybe three.

Not ten links. Not a page two to scroll. One name, maybe three.

Remember wish number one? Your name comes up when somebody asks?

That's what's at stake now.

And the AI can only go by what it can find about you. Your website. Your reviews. What your customers have said, in their own words. Same as a careful new customer would, except it reads all of it in a few seconds.

Which raises a fair question: what does it find?

You Can't See What's Missing

Here's the tricky part.

You can see what's on your website. You've looked at it a hundred times.

But you can't see what's missing. What should be there, and isn't.

Missing stuff doesn't show up as a blank box with a sign on it. It just doesn't show up.

Here's what I mean. Jamon runs a handyman and fence business here in Indiana. Before we met him, his website was TJOAT.net.

Anybody know what that stands for?

The Jack Of All Trades.

Nobody on earth would have figured that out. Jamon knew. His regulars knew. A stranger hearing it at a barbecue? No chance. And because Jamon could see the name every day, he couldn't see the problem.

That's the thing about being inside your own business. You're the one person who can never see it fresh.

But a stranger can. And AI is the most patient stranger you'll ever meet. It has never been in your shop. It doesn't know your regulars. It reads everything out there about you, and it tells you what a newcomer sees.

Before our November 9 night, we did this for a few of the businesses in the room. We looked them up the way a brand-new customer would. Some of what came back surprised us.

Then I asked the whole room to do it on their own phones. Here's the exact ask.

Steal This

Look yourself up. Four minutes. Type this into ChatGPT or Claude:

"Look up [my business] in [my town] the way a brand-new customer would. Tell me: what do customers love about us, in their words? What would a new customer want that they can't find? What's the one easy fix that's probably costing us customers? Be kind and specific."

Then ask a second one:

"Who are the best [what I do] in Fishers or Carmel? Top 5, and why."

Just getting started? Run it on a competitor and see who you're up against.

This one's private. It's just for you.

Did you show up in the top five? Did it find something customers love that you've never once put on your website?

Whatever it found, here's the good news: every one of those is fixable.

Fresh Beats Many

Here's one fix almost anybody can do tonight.

Most people read reviews before they pick a local business, and most of them want recent ones. (BrightLocal's 2026 consumer survey put it at 97% who read reviews, and 74% who look for ones from the last three months.)

So it's not how many reviews you have. It's how fresh they are.

Forty glowing reviews from 2023 are nice. One from last Tuesday might do more.

So let's get you a fresh one. Ask the AI this:

"Write a short, warm text I can send to a happy customer asking for a Google review. Make it sound like me. Under 40 words. Here's my review link: _."

Then pick one real customer who loves you.

And send it before you go to bed.

Notice the AI is just the pen here. You still pick the person. You still hit send. The genie grants, you decide.

The Nine-Dollar Name

Back to Jamon.

Remember TJOAT.net? Here's what we changed it to.

GottaCallJack.com.

Look what's packed into it. The action you want people to take: gotta call. His name: Jack. And the business. All in one phrase.

Someone hears it at a barbecue, and they still remember it a week later. He never has to say a phone number again.

What did that cost?

About nine bucks. A domain name.

The name got them found. Then the follow-up (the calls and texts that go out on their own) got them booked.

And within a couple of months, Jamon went from chasing work to booked six months out.

Now, did the name do all of that? No.

But it sure didn't hurt.

Same business. Same work. Same guys. The only thing that changed is how it shows up.

Back To The Genie

So here's where wish number one stands.

Your next customer is going to ask their phone who's best in town. That's the new exit. McDonald's didn't get picked for being the best burger. It got picked for being right there when someone was hungry.

The exit isn't on the highway anymore. It's in your customer's pocket.

Tomorrow morning, the genie still won't show up. But you don't need it to. You've got four minutes to look yourself up like a stranger, two minutes to ask one happy customer for a fresh review, and one question to ask about your name: would a stranger at a barbecue remember it?

Wish number one isn't a wish anymore. It's a to-do list.
